Understanding Cervical Cancer

What You Need to Know

Cervical cancer is one of the most preventable types of cancer. Understanding the risks and getting regular screenings can save lives.

What is Cervical Cancer?

Cervical cancer develops in the cells of the cervix — the lower part of the uterus that connects to the vagina. It's primarily caused by persistent infection with human papillomavirus (HPV).

Prevention is Possible

Regular Pap smear tests and HPV vaccination are highly effective in preventing cervical cancer. When detected early, the 5-year survival rate is over 90%.

Know the Symptoms

Early stages often have no symptoms. Warning signs include abnormal bleeding, unusual discharge, pelvic pain, or pain during intercourse. Regular screening is crucial.

When to Get Screened

Women should start Pap smear screening at age 21 and continue regularly until age 65. Your doctor may recommend more frequent screening based on your health history.

The Pap Smear Test

A Pap smear collects cells from the cervix to check for abnormalities. It's a simple, quick procedure that can detect precancerous changes before they become cancer.

HPV and Its Role

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is responsible for nearly all cases of cervical cancer. The HPV vaccine can prevent most cervical cancers when given before exposure.

Understanding Your Results

Cervical Cell Classification

CerviStage AI helps identify cellular changes. Results range from normal to carcinoma, with clear recommendations for next steps.

Normal Cells

No abnormalities detected. Continue with regular screening as recommended by your healthcare provider.

!

Benign Cellular Changes

Mild, non-cancerous changes. Usually not concerning. Follow-up screening in 12 months is typically recommended.

⚠

Low-Grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (LSIL)

Mild cellular abnormalities often caused by HPV. Many cases resolve on their own. Colposcopy may be recommended for evaluation.

!

High-Grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (HSIL)

More significant cellular changes that may progress to cancer if untreated. Immediate colposcopy with biopsy is typically recommended.

§

Invasive Carcinoma

Cancer cells are present. Immediate referral to an oncologist and further diagnostic testing is required for treatment planning.

Important: These classifications should be reviewed by a qualified healthcare professional. Only a doctor can make a definitive diagnosis and recommend appropriate treatment.
